Before the Breach

Every year, tens of thousands of software security holes are made public. Only a handful end up on CISA's list of holes known to be exploited. Can we spot that handful before it happens?

Scroll ↓

In 2026, 72,670 new security holes were published.

Each star is one. Security people call them CVEs: a public ID for one flaw in one piece of software.

Only about 1 in 855 ever gets added to CISA's list of known-exploited holes (KEV).

That's the rate we saw in 2024 and 2025. If 2026 is the same, about 85 of these stars will catch fire.

Defenders can't fix them all. The challenge: pick 500.

Patch the right 500 and most attacks have nowhere to land. Pick the wrong ones and the fires start anyway.

Traits of CVEs that end up on KEV

Out of every 1,000 holes published since 2018, about 6 were added to CISA KEV.

Some kinds are added to KEV about 3× more often.

Holes in enterprise software, network edge devices like firewalls and VPNs, flaws that need no login or let an attacker take over the machine, and anything with public attack code.

Scary-sounding isn't the same as on KEV.

WordPress plugins: 3 added to KEV out of 13,777. Cross-site scripting: 22 out of 31,018. Few of them reach the KEV list, although KEV leans toward enterprise and government software, so this is partly about the list, not only about attackers.

Today, teams pick with a simple rule: “Is there public attack code? Then the highest risk score.”

We tested it on 2024 and 2025, where we already know which 75 holes were added to CISA KEV. Each thread is one of them.

It caught 25%. It missed the rest.

19 caught, 56 missed.

Here are those 75 holes added to KEV.

31 had public attack code, so the simple rule could at least see them.

44 were blind spots: no attack code and a low risk score.

The simple rule caught 0 of them. It can't. Nothing it looks at says “danger”.

Our model caught 2 of the 44.

That's small, and we won't pretend it isn't. But zero to two is the first sign of a signal the usual tools don't have: the words.

What IBM Granite reads

One of the two: a FortiOS flaw from 2025. No public attack code. A low risk score. Granite reads the description…

…and recognises it.

It sounds more like a past KEV entry than 98% of 2025's holes. Its closest past attack: a Fortinet login bypass, exploited in 2022.

But 2026 is different.

Each square is one month: the typical risk score (EPSS) of new holes from each reporter. Brighter means a higher score.

EPSS jumped about 6× from the July 2026 snapshot (cause unknown).

That's 62% of the 2026 list. A cut-off tuned on past years would break here, so our model compares each hole only with others from the same month.

Some data looks magical. It's too good to be true.

Our honest model caught 21. Add a few columns from today's database and watch…

100% of holes added to KEV are marked “Analyzed” today, against 46% of the rest.

The database reviews KEV-listed holes first, so that field leaks the answer. On the day you'd have to choose, it wasn't filled in yet. We never use it.

Our 500 picks for 2026

Grouped by what the flaw lets an attacker do, from description keywords. Many holes fit several groups; each is shown once, in its first matching group. Brighter circles are higher on our list. Hover or tap one to read why IBM Granite says it's dangerous.

The honest result

Tested on the past two years, we caught 21 holes that were added to KEV, against 19 for the best simple rule. That's a small edge, and the uncertainty range says it could be luck. In the strictest test (train on 2024, test on 2025) we were one behind: 12 vs 13.

What we are sure of: the blind spots are real, 2026 shifted mid-year, and we didn't cheat.
